Chapter 5 · LLM Advanced

Optimizers: from SGD to Adam to Muon

Every optimizer turns noisy gradients into a step. Adam adds memory and a per-coordinate scale; Muon equalizes the singular values of the whole update.

13 sections · needs: AdamW with warmup and cosine, pretraining objective, scaling laws, ZeRO/FSDP, transformer blocks

01The setting

Every optimizer answers: which direction, and how far?

We minimize a loss over parameters using noisy minibatch gradients. The optimizer is the rule that turns past gradients into the next step.

02SGD and momentum

Momentum averages away the oscillation and keeps the drift

SGD uses u = g: memoryless, one learning rate for everything. Momentum adds a velocity so consistent directions accumulate.

Nesterov evaluates the gradient at the look-ahead point \(\theta - \eta\mu m\). In the common PyTorch form the update is \(g_t + \mu m_t\) instead of \(m_t\). Muon uses it.

03Adaptive methods

Dividing by a running RMS gives every coordinate its own step

Transformer gradients differ by orders of magnitude: rare-token embedding rows, LayerNorm gains, attention projections. Adaptive methods rescale each coordinate. Large history means a small step; rare features get big ones.

RMSProp (Hinton, 2012) is a crude diagonal preconditioner: it uses gradient magnitude as a proxy for curvature.

04Adam

Adam is momentum over RMSProp, with bias correction

Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2014) = momentum in the numerator + RMSProp in the denominator + bias correction.

Adam's step is scale-invariant: multiply the loss by 1000 and the updates do not change, which is why one learning-rate range (about \(10^{-4}\) to \(10^{-3}\)) works across very different models. When gradients are noisy, \(|\hat m| \ll \sqrt{\hat v}\) and the step shrinks automatically: a per-coordinate signal-to-noise ratio.

05AdamW

Decay must bypass the normalization

With SGD, an L2 penalty and direct decay are identical: \(\theta - \eta(g + \lambda\theta) = (1-\eta\lambda)\theta - \eta g\). With Adam they are not, and torch.optim.Adam(weight_decay=...) does the coupled version.

  • Every parameter decays at the same relative rate \(\eta\lambda\), so \(\lambda\) is a clean knob, less entangled with \(\eta\).
  • With Adam's roughly constant update size, decay creates an equilibrium weight norm, which keeps the effective learning rate of normalized layers in check.
  • Do not decay biases and norm gains, and often not embeddings. Use parameter groups.

06Stability knobs

Three knobs decide whether Adam spikes

LLM pretraining almost universally uses \(\beta_2 = 0.95\) (GPT-3, Llama and successors). The cost is a noisier denominator, so smaller batches often want a larger \(\beta_2\). Adam is scale-invariant in steady state, so clipping mostly changes the relative weight of one batch inside \(m\) and \(v\). Watch the gradient norm: a steady rise or constant clipping means LR, \(\beta_2\), init or logit growth needs attention.

07Code: AdamW

AdamW is five tensor ops, and it matches PyTorch

my_adamw.py
import math
import torch


class MyAdamW(torch.optim.Optimizer):
    def __init__(self, params, lr=1e-3, betas=(0.9, 0.999), eps=1e-8, weight_decay=1e-2):
        super().__init__(params, dict(lr=lr, betas=betas, eps=eps, weight_decay=weight_decay))

    @torch.no_grad()
    def step(self):
        for group in self.param_groups:
            lr, (b1, b2), eps, wd = group["lr"], group["betas"], group["eps"], group["weight_decay"]
            for p in group["params"]:
                if p.grad is None:
                    continue
                state = self.state[p]
                if not state:
                    state["t"] = 0
                    state["m"] = torch.zeros_like(p)
                    state["v"] = torch.zeros_like(p)
                state["t"] += 1
                t, m, v, g = state["t"], state["m"], state["v"], p.grad

                # Decoupled decay: applied to the weights, never routed through m or v.
                p.mul_(1 - lr * wd)

                m.lerp_(g, 1 - b1)
                v.mul_(b2).addcmul_(g, g, value=1 - b2)

                bc1 = 1 - b1 ** t
                bc2 = 1 - b2 ** t
                denom = (v.sqrt() / math.sqrt(bc2)).add_(eps)
                p.addcdiv_(m, denom, value=-lr / bc1)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    make = lambda: torch.nn.Sequential(torch.nn.Linear(10, 32), torch.nn.GELU(), torch.nn.Linear(32, 1))
    a = make()
    b = make()
    b.load_state_dict(a.state_dict())

    opt_a = MyAdamW(a.parameters(), lr=1e-2, betas=(0.9, 0.95), weight_decay=0.1)
    opt_b = torch.optim.AdamW(b.parameters(), lr=1e-2, betas=(0.9, 0.95), weight_decay=0.1)

    for _ in range(200):
        x, y = torch.randn(64, 10), torch.randn(64, 1)
        for model, opt in ((a, opt_a), (b, opt_b)):
            opt.zero_grad()
            torch.nn.functional.mse_loss(model(x), y).backward()
            opt.step()

    for pa, pb in zip(a.parameters(), b.parameters()):
        assert torch.allclose(pa, pb, atol=1e-6), (pa - pb).abs().max()
    print("MyAdamW matches torch.optim.AdamW")

Exercise: change p.mul_(1 - lr * wd) to g = g + wd * p (coupled L2), confirm it now matches torch.optim.Adam(weight_decay=0.1), and plot the per-group weight norms of both over a longer run.

08The memory bill

Optimizer state is 12 of the 16 bytes per parameter

8-bit Adam (Dettmers et al., 2021) stores \(m\) and \(v\) in 8 bits. Optimizer states have outliers across many orders of magnitude, so it quantizes in blocks of 2048 values, each with its own scale, using a non-linear dynamic map that spends more codes near zero. States are dequantized, updated in 32 bits, and requantized every step. Available in bitsandbytes.

09Beyond Adam

Lion, Sophia, Shampoo and SOAP change what the step is built from

SOAP (Vyas et al., 2024) notes that Shampoo is close to running Adam in the eigenbasis of its preconditioners. So it does that: rotate the gradient into the eigenbasis of \(L\) and \(R\), run Adam there, rotate back, and recompute the basis only every \(f\) steps. More stable than Shampoo, with fewer hyperparameters.

10Muon

Muon sets every singular value of the update to about 1

Muon (Keller Jordan et al., 2024) set records in the NanoGPT speedrun, then was scaled by Moonshot AI. For a 2D weight, the momentum buffer \(B = U\Sigma V^\top\) is dominated by a few loud directions. Muon uses \(O = UV^\top\) instead: same directions, all singular values 1. One view: steepest descent under the spectral norm (Bernstein and Jordan).

11Code: Muon

A Muon step is momentum, five matmuls and a scale

muon.py
import torch


def newton_schulz5(G: torch.Tensor, steps: int = 5, eps: float = 1e-7) -> torch.Tensor:
    a, b, c = 3.4445, -4.7750, 2.0315
    X = G.bfloat16()
    # Work in the wide orientation so X @ X.T is the smaller Gram matrix.
    transposed = X.size(0) > X.size(1)
    if transposed:
        X = X.T
    # Frobenius normalization bounds every singular value by 1, inside the polynomial's basin.
    X = X / (X.norm() + eps)
    for _ in range(steps):
        A = X @ X.T
        B = b * A + c * A @ A
        X = a * X + B @ X
    if transposed:
        X = X.T
    return X.to(G.dtype)


@torch.no_grad()
def muon_step(W, grad, buf, lr=0.02, momentum=0.95, weight_decay=0.1, nesterov=True):
    buf.mul_(momentum).add_(grad)
    update = grad.add(buf, alpha=momentum) if nesterov else buf
    O = newton_schulz5(update)
    # Moonlight scaling: makes update RMS comparable to AdamW so its lr/wd settings transfer.
    O = O * 0.2 * max(W.shape) ** 0.5
    W.mul_(1 - lr * weight_decay)
    W.add_(O, alpha=-lr)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    G = torch.randn(256, 128) @ torch.diag(torch.logspace(0, -3, 128))  # badly conditioned
    O = newton_schulz5(G).float()
    s_before = torch.linalg.svdvals(G)
    s_after = torch.linalg.svdvals(O)
    print(f"singular values before: max/min = {(s_before.max() / s_before.min()).item():.1f}")
    print(f"singular values after:  min={s_after.min().item():.2f} max={s_after.max().item():.2f}")
    # Directions with tiny singular values converge slower; the bulk should land near 1.
    assert 0.5 < s_after.median() < 1.3

In a real model put all hidden 2D matrices in a Muon group and everything else in an AdamW group. Reference implementations: Keller Jordan's Muon repository and torch.optim.Muon in recent PyTorch.

12Schedules

WSD and schedule-free both avoid fixing the end in advance

WSD (popularized by MiniCPM; DeepSeek uses step-based variants) is warmup, a constant stretch, then a rapid decay over the last 10 to 20% of steps. The decay phase is a natural place to switch to high-quality data (annealing), as Llama 3 does.

Schedule-free won the self-tuning track of AlgoPerf 2024. Switch the optimizer between train() and eval(). At LLM scale WSD is still the more common choice.

13Fair comparison

Most wins shrink under a fair comparison

An under-tuned AdamW with \(\beta_2 = 0.999\) is a straw man. An optimizer that is slightly worse at its best LR but robust across LRs may be the better engineering choice.

14Wrap-up

Check the ideas before the project

Common confusions

  • Adam with weight_decay is AdamW.

    No. torch.optim.Adam(weight_decay=...) adds L2 to the gradient, which then gets normalized; AdamW decays the weights directly.

  • Bias correction is a minor detail.

    At \(t=1\) with \(\beta_2 = 0.999\) the uncorrected second moment is 1000x too small. It matters most at the start, which is also when instability is most likely.

  • \(\epsilon\) is just for numerical safety.

    It also sets the scale below which Adam becomes momentum SGD.

  • Lower \(\beta_2\) is always more stable.

    Lower \(\beta_2\) reacts faster to gradient spikes, but its estimate is noisier; the trade-off is tied to batch size.

  • Muon replaces AdamW.

    Muon handles hidden 2D matrices; embeddings, the LM head and 1D parameters still use AdamW.

  • Newton-Schulz computes the exact orthogonal factor.

    Its coefficients trade exactness for speed; singular values land roughly in 0.7 to 1.2.

  • WSD and schedule-free are the same idea.

    WSD is a schedule with a short final decay; schedule-free removes the decay by averaging iterates.

Check yourself

  1. Derive why \(\mathbb{E}[v_t] = (1 - \beta_2^t)\,\mathbb{E}[g^2]\) under stationarity. What happens to the first Adam update if you skip bias correction on \(v\) but keep it on \(m\)?

    Show answer

    Unroll \(v_t = (1-\beta_2)\sum_{i=1}^t \beta_2^{t-i} g_i^2\), take expectations and sum the geometric series. Without correction, \(v_1 = 0.001\,g_1^2\) (for \(\beta_2 = 0.999\)), so \(\sqrt{v_1} \approx 0.032|g_1|\) while \(\hat m_1 = g_1\). The update is about \(31.6\,\eta\) per coordinate instead of \(\eta\): a huge first step.

  2. Explain why L2 regularization inside Adam decays a parameter with large historical gradients less than one with small historical gradients.

    Show answer

    The term \(\lambda\theta\) is added to \(g\), so the coordinate's update is about \((g + \lambda\theta)/\sqrt{\hat v}\). A large \(\hat v\) divides the decay term down; a small \(\hat v\) amplifies it.

  3. A 7B model is trained with mixed-precision AdamW. How many GB does model state take? How much would 8-bit Adam save?

    Show answer

    16 bytes/param \(\times 7\times10^9 \approx 112\) GB. 8-bit Adam cuts \(m\) and \(v\) from 8 bytes to 2 bytes per parameter, saving 6 bytes/param, about 42 GB (leaving about 70 GB).

  4. Why does LLM pretraining use \(\beta_2 = 0.95\) rather than 0.999?

    Show answer

    With 0.999, \(v\) averages over about 1000 steps and lags when a gradient suddenly grows, so updates become much larger than intended and the loss spikes. With 0.95 it averages over about 20 steps and tracks the change.

  5. What does Newton-Schulz do to the singular values and singular vectors of the momentum matrix, and why does that make a better update?

    Show answer

    Singular vectors are unchanged; every singular value is mapped to roughly 1. The update then moves equally in all directions of the momentum's column and row space instead of being dominated by a few large ones. That is steepest descent under the spectral norm, and it helps underrepresented directions learn.

  6. A paper shows optimizer X reaches AdamW's loss in 30% fewer steps on a 125M model. List three questions you would ask before adopting it.

    Show answer

    Was AdamW's LR, weight decay, \(\beta_2\) and warmup tuned as carefully? Does the gain hold in wall-clock time and at larger scales? Were finished schedules compared at equal budgets, with multiple seeds? (Also: memory overhead, stability across LRs.)

Chapter project

An optimizer bake-off on your GPT-2

Compare AdamW, Lion, Adafactor and Muon on one model, one dataset and one token budget, then decide what the evidence supports.

  1. Use GPT-2 small (or a 20 to 50M parameter version) with a fixed dataset and token budget, for example 500M tokens.
  2. Implement or import AdamW (your MyAdamW), Lion, Adafactor, and Muon (hidden 2D matrices) with AdamW on the rest.
  3. Sweep learning rate over at least 5 log-spaced values per optimizer with the same WSD schedule, warmup and decay fraction. Record final validation loss, tokens/sec and peak memory.
  4. Plot validation loss vs learning rate (the LR sensitivity curve), and loss vs wall-clock at each best LR.
  5. Re-run the best two at a model 2 to 4x larger. Does the gap grow or shrink?
  6. Stretch: from one WSD run, branch decays at 25%, 50% and 100% of the budget and compare with three separate cosine runs of those lengths.
  • A table plus a one-paragraph recommendation.
  • The recommendation states what you tuned and what you did not.
